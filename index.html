Original poem

Harry’s watching BoJack on my couch while I make honey dijon chicken.

He’s got his shoes up on my coffee table.

There’s nothing you can do about Harry. Back when we
were broken up, when we refused to speak to each other, I still
saw him everywhere: in pots, pans, hypoallergenic jewelry, solar panels,
even the upper section of the Chrysler building. He was the principal
dancer of our lives. When I got surgery on my middle
finger, Harry was there in the needles and forceps and the bolt
they screwed into my knuckle. And beyond all that, even
if I’d swapped all the steel in the world for titanium or aluminum
or durable plastics like polypropylene, it couldn’t change the eternal
fact that we shared the same birthday. Or that we both,
unbeknownst to the other, tried to reserve Cowgirl Lounge
for the very same February night. Or that we’d sent near-identical Partifuls
to a near-identical guest list, that we’d planned to wear
near-identical black long-sleeve square-neck Skims shirts and play
Big Thief albums on aux. At that point, it was all over.

In front of our family and friends, all the event staff, all the Partiful data agents,
we’d inadvertently proclaimed our sameness, our twin-soulness, spit out
the foolish arbitrary nature of our feud like false teeth.
At that point, we had no choice but to gum it up
together.

To lay down our weapons and grieve, to turn towards each other in sorrow, to
metabolize that sorrow into beauty and truth. To conjoin our birthday parties,
spend a collective $800 on Texas margaritas, to invite everyone’s
exes and let them figure it out, let them metabolize each other,
shepherd each other through the dark webby undernetting
to that silver light focused by love.
